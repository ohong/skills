# Evaluating a revision

Give the agent the request and input from one case. Keep the acceptance conditions aside until you have its answer. For a comparison, use the same model and request with and without the skill. Hide which version produced each revision. Judge each against the conditions, then identify the sentence that decided your preference. A tie is a valid result. A changed fact, invented detail, or stronger causal claim fails the case even if the prose reads better.

## 1. Turn field notes into a public notice

**Request.** Write a notice for library visitors from these notes.

**Input.** “Roof work Mon–Wed. Reading room only. Rest of branch normal hours. Books on hold normally collected in reading room. Those days → front desk. Contractor thinks room may reopen Thurs.”

**Pass when** a visitor can tell which room closes, when, and where to collect a hold. The rest of the branch keeps normal hours. Thursday remains tentative.

## 2. Let two audiences see the same mechanism

**Request.** Rewrite this for a new support hire. Then write it for the engineers on call.

**Input.** “An acknowledgment timeout triggers redelivery. The handler uses the event ID for deduplication.”

**Pass when** the new hire can explain why the same event may arrive twice and how the handler recognizes it. The engineering version keeps useful terms. Both distinguish a missing acknowledgment from a failure to process the event.

## 3. Follow the evidence

**Request.** Turn these notes into a paragraph for a school board.

**Input.** “Late arrivals lower at 4 of 6 schools in first month after bus change. Other two about same. Same month roadworks ended. No comparison group.”

**Pass when** the paragraph gives the denominator and time frame, accounts for the other schools, and explains why the change in arrivals cannot be attributed to the buses alone.

## 4. Edit a memory lightly

**Request.** Smooth the opening. Keep the narrator’s timing.

**Input.** “In relation to my earliest experiences of public speaking, I remember the microphone in the parish hall. I tapped it. My mother, three rows back, tapped her ear. Tough room.”

**Pass when** the reader reaches the microphone quickly. The mother’s gesture still precedes the punchline. The edit retains the narrator’s dry delivery and the source’s physical details.

## 5. Know when to leave a sentence alone

**Request.** Copyedit this exhibit label. Keep attention on the bowl.

**Input.** “The bowl was found in a well. Its rim still bears the marks of the cord it was lowered with.”

**Pass when** the bowl stays in focus, the finder remains unnamed, and the cord’s role stays clear. An unchanged label earns full credit.

## 6. Repair the order of an explanation

**Request.** Make this paragraph easier for a museum visitor to follow.

**Input.** “The second line is the part that matters. In summer the river drops. There are two blue lines on the ferry steps. The lower one marks the depth the ferry needs to float. The upper one shows the usual spring level. Below the lower line the ferry stays tied up.”

**Pass when** readers encounter the steps and their two lines before they must distinguish them. They can identify each mark and explain when the ferry stays tied up. The ending gives the marks a consequence.
