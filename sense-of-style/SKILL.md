# The Sense of Style

Treat the reader as an intelligent equal who has come to see something the writer can show them. In an explanation, that might be a mechanism. In a memoir, it might be the moment the narrator understood a joke.

1. **Find the piece.** Identify the reader, what they already know, and what they should understand, feel, or do by the end. Infer these from the material and the request. Notice what already works, especially a telling detail or a turn of phrase only this writer would use. Match the depth of the edit to the request.
2. **Give the reader a way in.** Find the subject beneath the throat-clearing. With raw notes, group observations around the question they answer and choose an order the reader can follow. A decision memo may open with the decision. A story can open with the odd detail that makes the reader want an explanation. Give each paragraph a job. At each break, check why the next paragraph belongs there.
3. **Supply the missing step.** Read from the audience’s side. Where would someone have to know the writer’s private shorthand, picture an unnamed object, or guess how one event led to another? Introduce an unfamiliar idea through an action or example the material supports, then give it a name if the reader will need it again. Keep the precise term when experts already think in it. Where the source leaves a consequential gap, preserve the uncertainty or ask a focused question.
4. **Guide attention through each sentence.** Start from an idea the reader has in mind and land on the new detail that matters. Repeat a key noun when a synonym suggests a second thing. Unpack strings of modifiers until the relationships are clear. Bring a subject close to its verb when an interruption forces a reread. Give a buried action its verb and name the actor when responsibility matters. A passive verb can keep the reader’s attention on the thing being changed.
5. **Read for the ear and check the meaning.** Listen for an unintended pause, a string of identical beats, or an ending that trails into padding. Give the telling word room to land. Keep an idiom, a fragment, or a long sentence when it suits the voice and reads cleanly. Settle disputed usage by meaning, audience, established usage, and any required house style. Compare the result with the source for facts, quotations, attribution, scope, and degree of certainty. Return the finished prose in the requested format. Include editorial notes when requested or when the author must resolve a material ambiguity.

## Two editorial decisions

**Recover the action.** “Following the removal of the footbridge, access to the orchard by the children required a walk to the ford.” → “Once the footbridge was gone, the children had to walk to the ford to reach the orchard.” The children have something to do. The sentence leaves open who removed the bridge.

**Keep the reveal.** “My father kept every receipt. Even the one for the shoebox he kept them in.” The fragment lands the joke. Joining the sentences or opening with a verdict about his thrift would spend the surprise early.
