# Writing clearly and concisely

1. **Identify the reader’s task.** Determine what the reader already knows and what they need to understand, decide, or do. Use that purpose to choose the opening and decide which details belong.
2. **Choose precise words.** Name the actual thing. Use the simplest word that carries the intended meaning. Keep familiar technical terms for specialists. For other readers, use an exact everyday equivalent or explain the term at first use. A short definition, a description of what it does, or a familiar comparison can supply the missing context.
3. **Make the action easy to find.** Put the person or thing doing the work near the main verb. Prefer active voice when the actor matters. Passive voice can keep attention on a test sample, describe an action whose actor is unknown, or spare someone needless embarrassment. Name the responsible party when the evidence identifies them and accountability matters. For a tangled sentence, use the repair below.
4. **Connect the sentences.** Start with something the reader recognizes and move toward the new information. Let that information become the starting point for the next sentence. Use a short sentence to emphasize a result. Join related clauses when the reader needs to see their relationship. Choose transitions that express the actual link, such as time, location, cause, similarity, or contrast.
5. **Check meaning after cutting.** Read the passage once in order. Replace any ambiguous “it,” “they,” or “this” with the intended noun. Cut repeated ideas, empty modifiers, and details the reader can do without. Check each claim against the source, including quantities, conditions, exceptions, uncertainty, and responsibility. Keep words that carry those distinctions.

For a draft or revision, deliver the finished text in the requested format and voice. For a review, quote the passage, explain what could confuse the reader, and suggest a specific edit.

## Repair a tangled sentence

Use Richard Lanham’s *paramedic method* when a sentence hides who did what.

1. Find the main verb and its subject. Look for empty openings such as “there is,” forms of “be,” and actions buried in nouns such as “evaluation.”
2. Recover the action. “The team conducted an evaluation of the filter” becomes “The team evaluated the filter.” Keep “is” when it states a condition directly, as in “The valve is closed.”
3. Unpack strings of nouns and prepositions. Translate “backup failure notification delay” into the relationship the source supports. Did the backup fail late, or did its failure notice arrive late? If the source leaves a material ambiguity, flag it.
4. Remove verbal padding. Replace “in the event that” with “if.” Express multiple negatives as a direct statement when the meaning holds. Preserve the force of a prohibition, a condition, or a qualified claim.

**Example.** “There was no initiation of a retry by the client due to the fact that no acknowledgment had been received” becomes “Because the client had received no acknowledgment, it did not retry.” The first clause gives the reason. The second names the action. Both preserve what the client knew.
